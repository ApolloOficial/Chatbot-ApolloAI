Why must PV systems be optimised for
different climates?
Global PV deployment is expanding rapidly into regions with climates once considered
too challenging for solar energy: deserts, tropical zones, and snow-rich regions. These environments offer strong PV
potential but introduce unique design, operation, and reliability challenges.

Why Climate-Specific Strategies Matter
Design Challenges:
Tailored, climate-specific system design and mitigation strategies are
essential for maximizing PV lifetime and return on investment.
Performance Risks:
Locations with high solar potential may experience harsh conditions, which worsen performance losses and
increase system stress and degradation risk.
Environmental Stressors:
Temperature extremes, UV irradiation, moisture, snow, sand, and biofilm
directly impact energy yield and reliability.

🌨   Cold & Snowy Climates
Snow accumulation can cause shading, increased soiling losses, and
heavy loads that deform module frames or introduce glass breakage. Ice
buildup can block light and add mechanical stress, while thawing cycles
pose a risk of water ingress and refreezing damage.
Snow and Ice Formation
Cold temperatures can embrittle encapsulants and solder joints, making
modules more prone to cracking. Large temperature swings between
day and night lead to thermal cycling, causing microcracks and
long-term reliability issues.
Low Temperatures and Thermal Cycling
At high altitudes, the increased UV exposure can degrade the backsheets
and encapsulants due to high irradiance and snow reflecting 80-90% in
the UV range.
High Irradiance and UV Exposure
Strong winds can cause snow drifts that lead to uneven loading,
increasing mechanical stress on modules and structures. Wind can also
contribute to cold-related mechanical fatigue in mounting systems.
Wind and Snow Drifts
Perform detailed snow load analysis, wind exposure evaluation, and seasonal irradiance studies. Local
weather and historical snow data can be used to anticipate accumulation patterns and drifts.
Best Practices and Mitigation Strategies
Modules should have reinforced frames, thicker glass, and flexible encapsulants to handle snow loads
and cold temperatures. UV-resistant materials are ideal to withstand high-altitude UV exposure.
Use high tilt angles and sufficient ground clearance to reduce snow accumulation, and employ robust
racking for snow and wind loads. Shading from snow must be considered when planning cell strings.

🌦   Hot & Humid Climates
Stressors and Typical Problems
Assess humidity, rainfall intensity, biofilm formation potential, and local irradiance using weather data
and field measurements to inform cleaning strategies and site-specific design.
Best Practices and Mitigation Strategies
Use moisture-resistant encapsulants, high-barrier backsheets, corrosion-resistant junction boxes, and
well-sealed edges to prevent water ingress, delamination, and corrosion in high-humidity climates.
Design for effective drainage and airflow to minimise moisture accumulation, using higher than optimal
tilt angles and robust racking to reduce water pooling and biofilm buildup while ensuring easy
maintenance.
High humidity worsens the impact of high temperatures, as heat is not
easily dissipated from the modules and water droplets can be
accumulated on solar panels. This combination accelerates degradation
mechanisms in PV systems.
High Humidity and High Temperatures
In regions with high humidity, dust particles adhere more easily and
strongly to module glass covers, which then require thorough cleaning.
Additionally, dew can also cause cementation of dust particles, making
them hard to remove by natural cleaning.
Enhanced Dust Adhesion
Organic growth is a unique challenge in humid climates, and can
negatively impact performance and increase maintenance costs. Beyond
the modules, components such as mounting structures, cables, and
junction boxes are also susceptible to biofouling.
Biological Growth
Tropical and subtropical locations can be characterised by rapid and
frequent variations in irradiance, due to frequent cloud cover changes,
which cause rapid, low-amplitude temperature changes in PV modules.

About IEA PVPS Task 13
Task 13 aims to enhance the quality, performance, and reliability of PV modules and
systems by summarizing technical aspects, gathering global data, and disseminating
results through reports, workshops, webinars, and web content. Task 13's expertise
ensures relevant analysis for stakeholders, contributing to technology advancement,

Standard modules are insufficient
In cases of limited knowledge about site-specific requirements and/or unavailability of
climate-specific PV modules, standard products are often deployed. Furthermore, the
push for cheaper modules has driven the trend toward larger PV modules with thinner
glass, cheaper encapsulants and backsheets, and reduced frame thickness, which are
nowadays increasing the degradation and failure rates in harsh environments.
Climate-specific materials must be deployed
Reinforced front glass, improved frame geometries, micro-crack-resistant
interconnections, and advanced encapsulants like POE or silicone enhance durability in
harsh conditions. Special coatings for soiling, heat, snow, or corrosion are being explored,
but further studies are needed to confirm their longevity and cost-effectiveness.
All solutions require thorough testing and study
Very often, mitigation measures aimed at addressing one issue can inadvertently
exacerbate another, making it essential to conduct thorough testing or gain a deeper
understanding of actual load conditions to identify the most effective solution.
Further data gathering will prove essential
Experience with climate-optimised PV modules is still limited, requiring more field data
and lessons learned to be exchanged within the PV community.
